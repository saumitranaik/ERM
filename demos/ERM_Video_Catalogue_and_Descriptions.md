# ERM Video Catalogue and Descriptions

This catalogue explains, in plain English, what each ERM demonstration video will show once it is generated. It covers one new, not-yet-generated overview video, followed by the 46 videos in the established production sequence tracked in the [Video Production Manifest](video-production-manifest.md), which remains the authoritative source for order, identifiers and status. Titles and numbers are copied from the manifest and the [Demonstration Workflow Catalogue](../docs/19-roadmap/01-demonstration-workflow-catalogue.md). The videos show a reference prototype built on a fictional company, “Meridian Asset Management Ltd.”, and ERM’s specifications; they do not show a finished production system. A video marked “Not Started” has no production package yet. Even where a rendered file exists, it has not been approved or published.

## New Overview Video

<a id="erm-summary-video"></a>
### ERM Summary Video (New — Not Yet Generated)

- **Identifier**: None assigned. It is deliberately outside the numbered sequence, and no sequence number is used or changed.
- **Status**: New — not yet generated. No production package, script or render exists.
- **Source note**: The repository defines no scope for this video, so the description below draws only on the repository’s README, CLAUDE.md and workflow catalogue.

Think of an organisation as a ship. Enterprise Risk Management (ERM) is the system that keeps a lookout, a logbook and a chain of command. This video will give a plain-English overview of what ERM is for: helping a regulated organisation, first a SEBI-regulated mutual fund management company in India, identify what could go wrong, judge how serious each risk is, decide what to do about it, and keep watching. It will show how that connects to everyday work through safeguards (controls) that are tested, named owners who are accountable, and approvals that need a second person. It will explain how rules from regulators, internal policies and audits are tracked in the same place, so leaders can make informed decisions with consistent processes and evidence behind them. It will be honest about maturity: the repository today contains detailed specifications and a demonstration prototype built on a sample company, not a finished production system.

## Established Sequence

Statuses below are taken from manifest §23 and §25. Videos 01–02 are Level 1 workflows; the manifest’s order is preserved exactly.

<a id="video-01"></a>
### Video 01 — Risk Assessment Approval

- **Identifier**: Video 01 (workflow L1-01)
- **Status**: Production package complete; rendered as Version 1 (silent demonstration, no spoken narration or music); QA complete for Version 1 scope only; approval not started; not published

Imagine a company keeps a list of everything that could go wrong, and each item gets a score for how likely and how damaging it is. This video will show a risk manager, Arjun, re-scoring one risk and sending it for sign-off, and the Chief Risk Officer, Priya, reviewing and approving it. The point is the “two-person rule”: the person who proposes a change is never the one who approves it. Viewers will see the risk change only after approval, with a permanent record of who decided what and when. It is the first demonstration of how every governed action in ERM works.

<a id="video-02"></a>
### Video 02 — Risk Treatment Plan Approval

- **Identifier**: Video 02 (workflow L1-02)
- **Status**: Production package complete; rendered as Version 1 (silent demonstration, no spoken narration or music); QA complete for Version 1 scope only; approval not started; not published

Once a risk is known, someone has to decide what to do about it: reduce it, accept it, hand it to someone else (like insurance), or avoid the activity altogether. This video shows the risk manager writing down that chosen plan for a specific risk and the Chief Risk Officer approving it. Viewers will see how the plan stays attached to the risk it addresses, so anyone later can see not just that a risk exists but what the organisation promised to do about it, and who agreed to that promise.

<a id="video-03"></a>
### Video 03 — Risk Acceptance

- **Identifier**: Video 03 (workflow L1-03)
- **Status**: Not Started

Sometimes a business decides a risk is not worth fixing and chooses to live with it. That is allowed, but it should be a formal, visible decision rather than a quiet shrug. This video will show a risk manager requesting that a risk be formally accepted and the Chief Risk Officer signing off, with an expiry so the decision must be revisited. Viewers will see why a recorded, time-limited “we accept this” is far safer than an unwritten one, especially when a regulator or auditor asks.

<a id="video-04"></a>
### Video 04 — KRI Breach → Escalation Acknowledgement

- **Identifier**: Video 04 (workflow L1-04)
- **Status**: Not Started

A Key Risk Indicator (KRI) is like a dashboard warning light: a number the business watches, such as failed transactions per day, with green, amber and red zones. This video will show a measurement landing in the red zone and the system automatically alerting a senior executive, who must acknowledge it. Viewers will see that warnings do not just sit on a screen; they reach a named person, and the acknowledgement is recorded, so nobody can later say they were never told.

<a id="video-05"></a>
### Video 05 — Control Test Execution & Result Capture

- **Identifier**: Video 05 (workflow L1-05)
- **Status**: Not Started

A control is a safeguard, like requiring two signatures on a payment. But a safeguard on paper proves nothing until someone checks that it works. This video will show a control owner, Sneha, recording a test of a control, whether its design is sound or whether it actually worked in practice, and the Head of Compliance, Deepak, approving the result. Viewers will see how evidence of checking is captured and kept, which is what auditors and regulators ask to see.

<a id="video-06"></a>
### Video 06 — Control Exception Lifecycle

- **Identifier**: Video 06 (workflow L1-06)
- **Status**: Not Started

What happens when a safeguard fails its test? This video will show a failed control test immediately raising an “exception”, a formal record that something is not working, which then moves through fixing the problem and a governed sign-off before it can be closed. Viewers will see that a failure cannot quietly disappear: it stays open and visible until someone with authority confirms it is genuinely resolved, with the whole history preserved.

### Video 07 — Compliance Assessment Approval

- **Identifier**: Video 07 (workflow L1-07)
- **Status**: Not Started

Regulators set obligations, meaning things the organisation must do, such as reporting within a deadline. This video will show a compliance officer, Kavitha, assessing whether the organisation is meeting one obligation, and the Head of Compliance approving that judgement. It also shows a built-in rule for “non-compliant” results, which cannot simply be waved through. Viewers will see how “are we following the rules?” becomes a reviewed, dated answer rather than an opinion.

Rules change: a regulator such as SEBI publishes a new circular, and the organisation must notice and respond. Viewers will see how the organisation keeps its list of “things we must do” current, and how an old requirement is marked as replaced instead of deleted.

When the organisation finds it is not meeting a rule, that gap is logged as an exception and a decision is needed on what to do with it. This video will show a compliance officer handling such an exception, with a rule that stops it being closed prematurely, and the Head of Compliance approving the outcome, including formally accepting the risk where appropriate. Viewers will see how compliance gaps are tracked to a clear, accountable resolution.

An attestation is a formal “I confirm” statement, like a manager signing to say their team followed the rules this quarter. This video will show a compliance officer submitting a periodic attestation and the Head of Compliance approving it. Viewers will see how these sign-offs create a dated, named record of responsibility, which is useful when a regulator wants proof that someone senior stood behind the organisation’s compliance claims.

Viewers will see how reporting is made easy, while closing the matter stays controlled, so incidents are neither hidden nor quietly dropped.

Fixing a symptom is not the same as fixing the cause. A root cause analysis asks “why did this really happen?” until the underlying reason is found. This video will show the Incident Manager drafting such an analysis for an incident or issue and the Chief Risk Officer approving it. Viewers will see why the organisation insists on a reviewed explanation of causes before deciding how to stop the problem returning.

### Video 21 — Issue → CAPA Full Lifecycle

- **Identifier**: Video 21 (workflow L1-21)
- **Status**: Not Started

This is the most detailed single-module story in the series. An issue, meaning a problem that needs tracking, is opened, a corrective and preventive action (a tracked fix plus a plan to prevent repeats) is created, carried out, and finally checked to see whether it actually worked. Three separate approvals guard the way. Viewers will see a problem followed from first report all the way to confirmed, reviewed resolution, which is the heart of how an organisation learns from mistakes.

Viewers will see that missed deadlines automatically climb to someone senior, rather than depending on a person to notice and speak up.
